# Endgames

Notes on endgame principles, basic checkmates and the theoretical positions
every player meets. Written for this app in its own words.

Sources the facts were checked against: Wikipedia's articles on chess
endgames and the named positions; Dvoretsky's Endgame Manual; Silman's
Complete Endgame Course; Syzygy tablebase results for the won/drawn claims.

## Endgame principles
Also: how to play the endgame; endgame tips; endgame rules
In the endgame the king becomes a fighting piece: bring it to the centre.
Passed pawns gain value and should be pushed when safe. Rooks belong behind
passed pawns. With an extra pawn or more, trade pieces, not pawns. Do not
hurry: improve everything first, then act. Remember that a lone bishop or
knight cannot mate, so the last pawns matter enormously.

## Active king
Also: king in the endgame; activate the king; centralize the king
With queens off, the king is a strong piece, worth roughly a minor piece in
fighting value. It should march toward the centre or toward the pawns, to
support its own passed pawns and attack the enemy's. Many endgames are won
simply because one king got to the action first.

## Opposition
Also: the opposition; direct opposition; taking the opposition
Two kings face each other on the same file or rank with one square between
them. The side that does not have to move "has the opposition": the other
king must give way. In king and pawn endings the opposition often decides
whether a pawn promotes or the game is drawn.

## Distant opposition
Also: distant opposition; long distance opposition
The kings stand on the same file or rank with an odd number of squares
between them (three or five). Taking the distant opposition early lets a
king turn it into direct opposition when it matters.

## Key squares
Also: key squares; critical squares
For a pawn, the key squares are the squares where, if the attacking king
reaches one, the pawn promotes whatever the defender does. For a pawn on the
fourth rank or below (not a rook's pawn), they are the three squares two
ranks in front of it; once the pawn reaches the fifth rank, the squares one
and two ranks ahead of it, on its own file and the two beside it, all become
key squares.

## King and pawn versus king
Also: king and pawn vs king; KP vs K; can a king and pawn win
The most basic endgame. The defender draws by getting the king in front of
the pawn and keeping the opposition; the attacker wins by getting the king in
front of the pawn, ideally two squares ahead, on a key square. A king on the
sixth rank in front of its pawn (not a rook's pawn) always wins. With a
rook's pawn, the defending king only needs to reach the corner.

## Rule of the square
Also: square of the pawn; can the king catch the pawn
A quick way to tell whether a king can catch a passed pawn without help: draw
a square from the pawn to its promotion rank (one side of the square runs
from the pawn to the eighth rank). If the defending king is inside the
square, or can step into it on its move, it catches the pawn. Remember that a
pawn on its starting square can move two.

## Triangulation
Also: triangulation; lose a tempo with the king
A king manoeuvre that takes three moves to return to the same square, using a
triangle of squares, so the same position arises with the opponent to move.
It is a way to "lose a tempo" and put the opponent in zugzwang when the
defending king has fewer squares available.

## Outside passed pawn
Also: outside passer; outside passed pawn
A passed pawn far from the other pawns, usually on the a- or h-file side. In
king and pawn endings it is often decisive: it lures the enemy king away to
stop it, and your king then eats the remaining pawns on the other side.

## Protected passed pawn
Also: protected passed pawn
A passed pawn defended by another pawn. The enemy king cannot take it, so it
ties the king down permanently, leaving your own king free to roam.

## Pawn breakthrough
Also: pawn breakthrough; breakthrough sacrifice; three pawns against three
With pawns facing each other, a sacrifice can create a passed pawn by force.
The classic case: white pawns on a5, b5, c5 against black pawns on a7, b7, c7
with the kings far away; White wins with b6!, and after ...axb6 c6! or
...cxb6 a6!, one pawn promotes.

## Zugzwang
Also: zugzwang; obligation to move; mutual zugzwang
German for "compulsion to move". A position where the side to move would be
better off passing, because every legal move makes the position worse. It is
common in endgames, especially king and pawn endings, and occasionally decides
middlegames. In mutual zugzwang, whoever has to move is worse off.

## Fortress
Also: fortress; build a fortress; impregnable position
A defensive setup the stronger side cannot break through, so the game is
drawn despite the material deficit. Examples: a rook and pawn against a queen
with the rook protected by the pawn near the king, or a bishop holding a
blockade on squares the enemy king cannot reach.

## Checkmate with king and queen
Also: king and queen vs king; mate with a queen; KQ vs K; how to checkmate with a queen
Use the queen to restrict the enemy king to a shrinking box, a knight's move
away from it, then bring your own king close and mate on the edge. Always
check that the enemy king has a move, because stalemate is the classic
mistake here. It can always be done in at most ten moves.

## Checkmate with king and rook
Also: king and rook vs king; mate with a rook; KR vs K; how to checkmate with a rook
Use the rook to cut the king off on a rank or file, bring your king up to
take the opposition, then check along the edge to push the defending king
back a rank at a time. Mate happens on the edge with the kings facing each
other. It can always be done in at most sixteen moves.

## Checkmate with two rooks
Also: two rook mate; ladder mate; lawnmower mate
The easiest mate: the two rooks take turns checking along ranks (or files),
each one cutting off the rank behind, walking the king to the edge like a
ladder. No help from the king is needed. Keep the rooks far from the enemy
king so it cannot attack them.

## Checkmate with two bishops
Also: two bishop mate; mate with two bishops; KBB vs K
Possible against a lone king. The bishops work side by side to form a barrier
that the king cannot cross, the attacking king helps, and the defending king
is driven into a corner, where it is mated. It takes some practice.

## Checkmate with bishop and knight
Also: bishop and knight mate; bishop and knight checkmate; KBN vs K; how to mate with bishop and knight
The hardest basic mate, possible in at most 33 moves from any position. The
king can only be mated in a corner the bishop's colour controls. The usual
technique is the W manoeuvre (Deletang's method), where the knight walks in
a W pattern to drive the king from the wrong corner to the right one. Many
masters have failed to do it within the fifty-move limit.

## Two knights cannot force mate
Also: two knights vs king; can two knights checkmate
Two knights and a king cannot force checkmate against a lone king: a mate
position exists, but the defender can always avoid it. Strangely, with an
extra pawn on the defending side, two knights can sometimes win, because the
pawn's moves remove the stalemate defence (the Troitzky line).

## Insufficient mating material in endgames
Also: lone bishop; lone knight; can a bishop checkmate
A king and a single bishop or a single knight cannot checkmate a lone king,
so those positions are drawn automatically. King and bishop against king and
bishop with both bishops on the same colour is also a draw. See the rules
notes on insufficient material for the exact cases.

## Wrong rook pawn
Also: wrong bishop; wrong colored bishop; rook pawn and bishop
King, bishop and a rook's pawn (a- or h-pawn) cannot win if the bishop does
not control the promotion square and the defending king reaches that corner:
the king can never be driven out, and it ends in stalemate. With the "right"
bishop, the pawn usually wins.

## Lucena position
Also: Lucena; building a bridge; rook endgame winning technique
The most important winning position in rook endings: the attacker's king is
in front of its own pawn on the seventh rank (not a rook's pawn), the
defending king is cut off on the side, and the defending rook checks from
behind. The win comes by "building a bridge": the attacker's rook goes to the
fourth rank to block the checks with the rook once the king steps out. Named
after Luis Ramírez de Lucena, though it first appears in a later book by
Salvio.

## Philidor position
Also: Philidor; third rank defense; rook endgame drawing technique
The key drawing technique in rook and pawn against rook: the defending king
stands in front of the pawn, and the defending rook holds the third rank (the
sixth from White's side, when defending as Black) so the attacking king
cannot advance. When the pawn moves up to the third rank, the rook drops to
the first rank and checks the king from behind. Described by Philidor in
1777.

## Rook endgames
Also: rook endings; all rook endings are drawn; rook endgame
The most common endgames in practice. Tarrasch's quip that "all rook endings
are drawn" exaggerates a real truth: an extra pawn is often not enough. Key
ideas: keep your rook active, even at the cost of a pawn; put rooks behind
passed pawns (Tarrasch's rule); cut off the enemy king; and know the Lucena
and Philidor positions.

## Rook behind the passed pawn
Also: Tarrasch rule; rook behind passed pawn; rooks belong behind passed pawns
Siegbert Tarrasch's rule: rooks belong behind passed pawns, your own or the
enemy's. Behind your own pawn, the rook gains activity as the pawn advances;
behind the enemy's pawn, it attacks the pawn and gains activity as the pawn
goes forward, while a rook in front gets more and more passive.

## Vancura position
Also: Vancura; rook against rook and rook pawn
A drawing method when the attacker has a rook and a rook's pawn on the sixth
rank, with the rook in front of it: the defending rook attacks the pawn
sideways along the same rank, while the defending king waits on the other
wing (on g7, against a white a-pawn). The attacking rook stays tied to the pawn, and when the king
comes over to help, the defending rook checks it from behind.

## Queen versus pawn on the seventh
Also: queen vs pawn; queen against pawn on seventh
A queen usually beats a pawn on its seventh rank: by checks and pins, the
queen forces the defending king in front of the pawn, which gains a tempo for
the attacking king to come closer. The exceptions are a rook's pawn or a
bishop's pawn, where the defender can draw by stalemate tricks if the
attacking king is too far away.

## Queen versus rook
Also: queen vs rook; Philidor queen vs rook
A queen beats a lone rook, but the technique is hard: the aim is to reach a
zugzwang where the rook must leave its king and get forked or skewered by the
queen. Philidor analysed the key position in the 18th century. A rook kept
close to its king can resist for a long time, so the winning side must be
precise.

## Opposite-coloured bishop endgames
Also: opposite colored bishops endgame; bishops of opposite colors drawn
With only bishops of opposite colours left, the defender can often draw even
two or three pawns down, because the defending bishop blockades the pawns on
squares the attacking bishop cannot contest. Winning chances improve if the
attacker has two widely separated passed pawns.

## Same-coloured bishop endgames
Also: same colored bishops endgame; bishop endgame
Endgames with bishops on the same colour are far more decisive. Key ideas:
put your pawns on squares the opposing bishop cannot attack, use the king
actively, and look for zugzwang. A "bad" bishop, hemmed in by its own pawns,
can lose by itself.

## Knight endgames
Also: knight endings; knight endgame; pawn endings with knights
Knight endings play a lot like king and pawn endings: the extra pawn usually
wins, and a far-away passed pawn is very strong because knights are slow to
cross the board. A knight struggles most against a rook's pawn.

## Bishop versus knight endgames
Also: bishop vs knight endgame; good bishop vs bad knight
The bishop is usually better with pawns on both wings and an open position,
because of its long range. The knight is better when pawns are fixed on the
bishop's colour, or all on one side. Each side tries to steer the structure
to suit its own piece.

## Rook versus bishop
Also: rook vs bishop; rook against bishop
Usually a draw without pawns. The defender should keep the king in the corner
that the bishop does not control, the "safe corner". In the wrong corner,
the rook side can sometimes win.

## Rook versus knight
Also: rook vs knight; rook against knight
Usually a draw without pawns if the knight stays close to its king and away
from the edges and corners. A knight separated from its king can be trapped
and lost.

## Réti's study
Also: Reti study; Reti endgame study; Réti manoeuvre
A famous 1921 study by Richard Réti: White's king, far from the black pawn and
apparently unable to catch it, walks diagonally to chase the pawn and support
its own pawn at the same time, and draws. It shows that a diagonal king walk
can pursue two goals at once.

## Endgame tablebases
Also: tablebase; tablebases; syzygy; Nalimov; perfect play endgame
Databases that give perfect results for every position with few pieces, now
complete up to seven pieces (both kings included). They proved that some
positions need hundreds of moves to win, longer than the fifty-move rule
allows. Engines and online analysis use them to play endings perfectly.

## Converting an extra pawn
Also: winning with an extra pawn; up a pawn in the endgame
Trade pieces but keep pawns, since every pawn exchange brings the defender
closer to a draw. Activate the king, create a passed pawn from your majority,
and use it to stretch the defence. In rook endings, an extra pawn wins less
often than in king and pawn or knight endings.
