# History

Notes on the history of chess: where it came from, how the rules took their
modern form, the world champions, famous players and games, and chess
computers. Written for this app in its own words. Facts about current events
are as of October 2026.

Sources the facts were checked against: Wikipedia's articles on the history
of chess, the World Chess Championship, the individual champions and famous
games, Scachs d'amor, the Candidates Tournament 2026 and World Chess
Championship 2026; FIDE's announcements; ChessBase and chess.com news reports.

## Origins of chess
Also: who invented chess; how old is chess; where did chess come from; chaturanga; history of chess
Chess descends from chaturanga, played in India by around the 6th century
AD. Its name refers to the four divisions of an ancient army: infantry,
cavalry, elephants and chariots, which became the pawns, knights, bishops
and rooks. No single inventor is known; legends such as the grains of wheat
on the chessboard are later stories. From India the game spread to Persia,
where it became shatranj.

## Shatranj
Also: shatranj; Persian chess; Arab chess; medieval chess
Shatranj was the form of chess played in Persia and then across the Islamic
world from about the 7th century. The pieces were weaker than today: the
ferz (the ancestor of the queen) moved one square diagonally and the alfil
(the bishop) jumped two squares diagonally, so games were slow. Strong
players wrote the first chess problems and books. The words "check" and
"checkmate" come from the Persian "shah" (king) and "shah mat".

## Chess comes to Europe
Also: medieval chess Europe; how chess came to Europe; Lewis chessmen
Chess reached Europe through Muslim Spain, Sicily and trade routes by around
the 10th century, and spread among the nobility. The famous Lewis chessmen,
carved from walrus ivory in the 12th century, were found on the Scottish
Isle of Lewis in 1831. In Europe the ferz became the queen, but it was still
a weak piece for centuries.

## Modern chess and the mad queen
Also: when did the queen get so strong; queen's move history; mad queen chess; Scachs d'amor; modern rules
Around the late 1400s, the queen and bishop gained their modern long-range
moves, transforming the game into the fast, sharp chess we know; at the time
it was nicknamed "the queen's chess" or "mad queen chess". The earliest known
description is the Valencian poem Scachs d'amor from about 1475. The first
printed book on the new game appeared in Valencia in 1495, and Lucena's book
followed in 1497. The two-square pawn move dates from the same period.

## History of castling
Also: when did castling start; castling history; king's leap
Castling grew out of the medieval "king's leap", which let the king make one
special jump early in the game. Many local versions of castling existed in
the 1400s and 1500s, and the modern form became standard in the early 1600s,
first in France and then in England. Some variants, like Italian free
castling, lasted into the 1800s.

## Ruy López de Segura
Also: Ruy Lopez the person; Ruy López; Spanish priest
A Spanish priest who was one of the strongest players of the 16th century.
His 1561 book gave the Ruy Lopez opening its name and recommended sitting so
the sun shines in the opponent's eyes. In 1575, at the court of Philip II of
Spain, he lost matches to the Italians Leonardo da Cutri and Paolo Boi, one
of the first international chess contests.

## Gioachino Greco
Also: Greco; Gioachino Greco
An Italian player of the early 1600s whose collection of games, many of them
probably composed, taught attacking patterns for generations. Several basic
traps and mating ideas, including the Greek gift sacrifice, appear in his
work.

## Philidor
Also: Philidor; François-André Danican Philidor; pawns are the soul of chess
François-André Danican Philidor (1726-1795) was the strongest player of the
18th century and also a successful opera composer. His 1749 book,
Analyse du jeu des échecs, stressed the importance of pawns: "pawns are the
soul of chess". He gave blindfold exhibitions that amazed his contemporaries.
The Philidor Defense and the Philidor position in rook endings are named
after him.

## The Romantic era
Also: romantic chess; romantic era; 19th century chess
Chess in the early and mid 1800s favoured bold attacks, gambits such as the
King's Gambit and Evans Gambit, and spectacular sacrifices, often at the cost
of soundness. Declining a gambit was considered unsporting. Adolf Anderssen
and Paul Morphy were its greatest figures; Wilhelm Steinitz's positional
ideas later brought the era to an end.

## Adolf Anderssen
Also: Anderssen; Adolf Anderssen
A German mathematics teacher, winner of the first international tournament
(London 1851) and regarded as the world's leading player in the 1850s and
1860s, apart from Morphy's brief reign. He played the Immortal Game and the
Evergreen Game, two of the most famous attacking games ever.

## Paul Morphy
Also: Morphy; Paul Morphy
An American prodigy from New Orleans (1837-1884), widely seen as the strongest
player of his time. In 1857 and 1858 he won the First American Chess Congress
and then crushed the leading European players, including Anderssen, before
retiring from serious chess while still in his early twenties. He understood
development and open lines far better than his rivals. His Opera Game is
still used to teach the value of development.

## The Immortal Game
Also: Immortal Game; Anderssen Kieseritzky 1851
Adolf Anderssen against Lionel Kieseritzky, London 1851, a casual game in the
King's Gambit. Anderssen sacrificed a bishop, both rooks and finally his queen,
then mated with his three remaining minor pieces. It is the symbol of the
romantic era's attacking style.

## The Evergreen Game
Also: Evergreen Game; Anderssen Dufresne 1852
Adolf Anderssen against Jean Dufresne, Berlin 1852, an Evans Gambit. Anderssen
ended it with a queen sacrifice and a forced mate with his two bishops. Wilhelm
Steinitz called it "the evergreen in Anderssen's laurel wreath".

## The Opera Game
Also: Opera Game; Morphy Opera game; Morphy Duke of Brunswick
Paul Morphy against the Duke of Brunswick and Count Isouard, played in a box
at the Paris Opera in 1858 during a performance. Morphy developed quickly,
sacrificed material to keep the initiative, and mated on move 17 with a rook
supported by a bishop, after a queen sacrifice. It is the classic lesson in
rapid development and open lines.

## The World Chess Championship
Also: world championship; world chess champion; list of world champions; who are the world champions
The official world championship began in 1886 when Wilhelm Steinitz beat
Johannes Zukertort. The undisputed champions: Steinitz, Lasker, Capablanca,
Alekhine, Euwe, Botvinnik, Smyslov, Tal, Petrosian, Spassky, Fischer, Karpov,
Kasparov, then (after a split and reunification) Kramnik, Anand, Carlsen,
Ding Liren and Gukesh Dommaraju. From 1993 to 2006 the title was split
between FIDE and the "classical" line of Kasparov and Kramnik. Since 1948
FIDE has organised the championship.

## Current world champion
Also: who is the world champion; current champion; Gukesh; world champion now; 2026 world championship
As of October 2026, the world champion is Gukesh Dommaraju of India, who beat
Ding Liren in Singapore in December 2024 and became, at 18, the youngest
undisputed champion ever. His challenger is Javokhir Sindarov of Uzbekistan,
who won the 2026 Candidates Tournament in Cyprus with an unbeaten 10 out of
14. Their match is scheduled for 24 November to 12 December 2026 in Geneva;
both players will be 20, the youngest pairing in championship history.

## Wilhelm Steinitz
Also: Steinitz; first world champion; first world chess champion
The first official world champion (1886-1894), born in Prague and later an
American citizen. He founded positional chess: accumulating small advantages,
the value of a sound pawn structure, and defending before attacking. His
ideas ended the romantic era and still underpin modern strategy.

## Emanuel Lasker
Also: Lasker; Emanuel Lasker
World champion for 27 years (1894-1921), the longest reign in history. A
German mathematician and philosopher, he was famous for his practical,
psychological approach: choosing moves that were hardest for the particular
opponent. Lasker's rule in rook and pawn endings and the Lasker Defense are
named after him.

## José Raúl Capablanca
Also: Capablanca; Capa; Jose Raul Capablanca
A Cuban genius, world champion 1921-1927, renowned for effortless positional
play and endgame technique; he lost only a handful of serious games in a
decade. Capablanca beat Lasker for the title in Havana and lost it to Alekhine
in 1927. His book Chess Fundamentals is still recommended.

## Alexander Alekhine
Also: Alekhine; Alexander Alekhine
A Russian-born French player, world champion 1927-1935 and 1937-1946, the only
champion to die holding the title. He was a fierce attacker with deep
combinative vision and huge preparation. He lost the title to Max Euwe in
1935 and won it back two years later. The Alekhine Defense is his.

## Max Euwe
Also: Euwe; Max Euwe
A Dutch mathematics teacher, world champion 1935-1937. He later served as
FIDE president from 1970 to 1978, steering chess through the Fischer era.

## Mikhail Botvinnik
Also: Botvinnik; Soviet school; patriarch
World champion 1948-1957, 1958-1960 and 1961-1963, the founder of the Soviet
chess school, whose scientific approach to preparation and training shaped
generations. He won the 1948 tournament held after Alekhine's death, and his
school trained Karpov, Kasparov and Kramnik.

## Vasily Smyslov
Also: Smyslov
Soviet world champion 1957-1958, famed for harmonious, natural play and
endgame skill. He beat Botvinnik for the title and lost the rematch a year
later. He was still a candidate for the title at 62.

## Mikhail Tal
Also: Tal; magician from Riga
The "Magician from Riga", world champion 1960-1961, the most daring attacker
of the modern era, whose sacrifices were often unsound but impossible to
refute over the board. At 23 he was then the youngest champion. He lost the
rematch to Botvinnik, partly due to poor health, and later set records with
long unbeaten streaks.

## Tigran Petrosian
Also: Petrosian; Iron Tigran
Soviet-Armenian world champion 1963-1969, a master of defence and
prophylaxis, almost impossible to beat. He was famous for positional exchange
sacrifices and for sensing danger before it appeared.

## Boris Spassky
Also: Spassky; Boris Spassky
Soviet world champion 1969-1972, a universal player equally at home in
attack and defence. He is best remembered for losing the 1972 match in
Reykjavik to Bobby Fischer and for his sportsmanship during it.

## Bobby Fischer
Also: Fischer; Bobby Fischer; Fischer Spassky 1972; match of the century
The American Bobby Fischer won the world title in 1972 in Reykjavik, beating
Boris Spassky in a Cold War "match of the century". He had crushed the
qualifying matches, including two 6-0 results. He refused to defend the title
in 1975, losing it to Karpov by default, and withdrew from competitive chess.
He also invented the increment clock and Chess960.

## Game of the Century
Also: Game of the Century; Byrne Fischer 1956
Donald Byrne against 13-year-old Bobby Fischer, New York 1956. Fischer gave up
his queen with a brilliant sacrifice and his minor pieces then hunted down
White's position and king, ending in mate. The chess writer Hans Kmoch named it
"the Game of the Century".

## Anatoly Karpov
Also: Karpov; Anatoly Karpov
Soviet world champion 1975-1985 and FIDE champion 1993-1999. A positional
master who slowly squeezed opponents with deep understanding and
prophylaxis, he won more tournaments than almost anyone. His five world
championship matches against Kasparov are among the most famous rivalries in
sport.

## Garry Kasparov
Also: Kasparov; Garry Kasparov
World champion from 1985, when at 22 he beat Karpov to become the youngest
champion of his time, until 2000. Many consider him the greatest player ever:
he was world number one for most of two decades, combining dynamic attacking
play with unmatched preparation. He lost to Deep Blue in 1997 and to Kramnik
in 2000, retired in 2005 and became a political activist.

## Kasparov's Immortal
Also: Kasparov Topalov 1999; Kasparov's immortal
Garry Kasparov against Veselin Topalov, Wijk aan Zee 1999. Kasparov sacrificed
a rook on d4 and drove Topalov's king across the board in a long, deep
combination. It is often called the greatest game of the modern era.

## Vladimir Kramnik
Also: Kramnik; Vladimir Kramnik
A Russian world champion who beat Kasparov in London in 2000, neutralising
him with the Berlin Defense. He reunified the title by beating FIDE champion
Veselin Topalov in 2006, then lost it to Anand in 2008.

## Viswanathan Anand
Also: Anand; Vishy Anand; Vishy
India's first grandmaster (1988), FIDE world champion in 2000 and undisputed
world champion from 2007 to 2013, famed
for his speed and universal style. His success inspired a chess boom in
India that produced a generation of top players, including Gukesh.

## Magnus Carlsen
Also: Carlsen; Magnus; Magnus Carlsen
A Norwegian, world champion 2013-2023, who beat Anand in Chennai and defended
the title four times. He reached the highest rating ever, 2882, in 2014, and
went 125 classical games unbeaten. He declined to defend the title in 2023,
saying he lacked motivation for another match, and remains the world's
top-rated player and dominant in rapid, blitz and Freestyle chess.

## Ding Liren
Also: Ding; Ding Liren
China's first men's world champion, who won the title in 2023 in Astana by
beating Ian Nepomniachtchi in rapid tiebreaks, after Carlsen declined to
defend it. He lost it to Gukesh in Singapore in December 2024, in the final
game of the match.

## Gukesh Dommaraju
Also: Gukesh; D Gukesh; Gukesh D
An Indian grandmaster born in 2006, who won the 2024 Candidates Tournament at
17 and then beat Ding Liren to become world champion at 18, the youngest
undisputed champion in history. He was trained in the Chennai chess scene
that grew from Anand's success.

## Javokhir Sindarov
Also: Sindarov; Javokhir Sindarov
An Uzbek grandmaster born in 2005, who won the 2026 Candidates Tournament in
Cyprus with a record unbeaten 10 out of 14, a round to spare. He challenges
Gukesh for the world title in Geneva in late 2026.

## Women in chess
Also: women's world champion; Judit Polgar; Hou Yifan; Vera Menchik; strongest female chess player
Vera Menchik was the first women's world champion, from 1927 until her death
in 1944. Judit Polgar is the strongest woman ever: she became a grandmaster at
15, reached the world top ten, and beat several world champions; she never
played in women-only events. Hou Yifan of China won the women's title four
times. Ju Wenjun of China has held the women's world title since 2018,
defending it for the fifth time in April 2025.

## Aron Nimzowitsch
Also: Nimzowitsch; My System
A Latvian-born grandmaster (1886-1935), leader of the hypermodern school.
His book My System (1925) introduced concepts like prophylaxis, overprotection
and the blockade, and remains one of the most influential chess books ever
written. The Nimzo-Indian Defense is his.

## FIDE
Also: FIDE; world chess federation; Fédération Internationale des Échecs
The International Chess Federation, founded in Paris in 1924. It sets the
Laws of Chess, organises the world championship and the Chess Olympiad,
publishes ratings and awards titles. Its motto is "Gens una sumus", Latin for
"We are one people".

## The Chess Olympiad
Also: Chess Olympiad; olympiad
A team competition between nations held every two years, with open and
women's sections. The first official Olympiad was in London in 1927. The
Soviet Union dominated it for decades.

## The Turk
Also: Mechanical Turk; the Turk; chess automaton
A famous fake chess-playing machine built by Wolfgang von Kempelen in 1770. It
toured Europe and America for decades, playing opponents including, by
legend, Napoleon and Benjamin Franklin. It was a hoax: a strong human player
hid inside the cabinet.

## Early chess computers
Also: chess programs history; Shannon; Turing; first chess program
Alan Turing wrote a chess algorithm by hand around 1950 and played it with
pencil and paper. Claude Shannon's 1950 paper set out how a computer could play
chess by searching moves and evaluating positions, the basis of engines for
decades. Programs grew steadily stronger, beating masters by the 1980s.

## Deep Blue
Also: Deep Blue; Kasparov Deep Blue; computer beat world champion
IBM's chess supercomputer. Kasparov beat it in 1996, but in a 1997 rematch in
New York Deep Blue won 3.5 to 2.5, the first time a computer beat a reigning
world champion in a match under tournament conditions. Kasparov accused the
team of human help, which IBM denied; the machine was retired afterwards.

## Modern chess engines
Also: Stockfish; AlphaZero; Leela; chess engines; best chess engine; NNUE
Today's engines far outplay any human. Stockfish, free and open source, has
been the strongest for years; since 2020 it uses NNUE, a small neural network
for evaluation. In 2017 DeepMind's AlphaZero taught itself chess from the
rules alone and beat Stockfish in a match, playing a striking, dynamic style;
Leela Chess Zero is an open-source project built on the same idea. In this
app, Stockfish calculates the moves and the analysis.

## Online chess and the chess boom
Also: online chess; chess boom; Queen's Gambit show; lichess; chess.com
Online chess grew steadily from the 1990s and exploded in 2020, when the
pandemic and the Netflix series The Queen's Gambit drew millions of new
players. Chess.com and Lichess, a free and open-source site, host millions of
games a day, and streamers made chess popular entertainment.

## Kasparov versus the World
Also: Kasparov versus the World; Kasparov vs the World 1999
In 1999 Garry Kasparov played a game online against the World, whose moves
were decided by vote, with advice from strong young players. Kasparov won
after 62 moves and four months, and later called it one of the greatest games
he had played.
