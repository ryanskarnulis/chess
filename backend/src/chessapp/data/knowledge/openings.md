# Openings

Notes on the openings: what each one is, its main ideas and typical plans.
Written for this app in its own words. Opening names follow the Lichess
chess-openings data set (CC0), the same names the app reads off the board.

Sources the facts were checked against: the Lichess chess-openings data set;
Wikipedia's articles on each opening; chess.com and chessable opening
overviews; Modern Chess Openings (15th edition) for move orders.

## King's Pawn Game
Also: 1. e4; king's pawn opening; e4 openings; open games; double king pawn
1. e4 is the most popular first move in chess. The pawn takes the centre,
opens lines for the queen and the light-squared bishop, and invites direct,
tactical play. Bobby Fischer called it "best by test". Black's main answers
are 1...e5 (the open games), 1...c5 (the Sicilian), 1...e6 (the French),
1...c6 (the Caro-Kann), 1...d5 (the Scandinavian) and 1...d6 or 1...g6 (the
Pirc and Modern). After 1. e4 e5 the game usually goes 2. Nf3 Nc6 and then
the Italian, the Ruy Lopez, the Scotch or the Four Knights.

## Italian Game
Also: Giuoco Piano; quiet game; 3. Bc4; Italian opening
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4. One of the oldest openings, analysed by Italian
players in the 1500s and 1600s. The bishop points at f7, the weakest square
in Black's camp, because only the king guards it. After 3...Bc5 (the Giuoco
Piano, "quiet game") White can play c3 and d4 to build a big centre, or the
slow Giuoco Pianissimo with d3, keeping tension and manoeuvring with Nbd2-f1-g3.
Modern grandmasters play the slow version a lot. Good first opening: natural
development, quick castling, clear plans.

## Italian Game: Two Knights Defense
Also: two knights; 3...Nf6; Prussian defence
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4 Nf6. Black counterattacks the e4 pawn instead of
copying with ...Bc5. White's most aggressive try is 4. Ng5, hitting f7 with
knight and bishop; after 4...d5 5. exd5 Black usually plays 5...Na5, giving
up a pawn for quick development and attacking chances. The quieter 4. d3
leads to Giuoco Pianissimo-style positions. Recapturing 5...Nxd5 instead
allows the Fried Liver Attack.

## Italian Game: Two Knights Defense, Fried Liver Attack
Also: Fried Liver; Fegatello; Nxf7 sacrifice
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4 Nf6 4. Ng5 d5 5. exd5 Nxd5? 6. Nxf7! White
sacrifices the knight on f7, dragging the king out with 6...Kxf7 7. Qf3+ Ke6,
and attacks the pinned knight on d5 with Nc3. The king is stuck in the centre
and White's attack is very dangerous, especially in fast games. Black avoids
it with 5...Na5. The Italian name Fegatello means roughly "piece of liver".
A favourite trap at club level.

## Italian Game: Evans Gambit
Also: Evans gambit; 4. b4
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4 Bc5 4. b4. White offers the b-pawn to deflect the
bishop, then gains time with c3 and d4 for a big centre and a fast attack.
Named after Captain William Evans, a Welsh sea captain, in the 1820s. It was
a favourite of the romantic era: Anderssen's Evergreen Game began with it.
Kasparov revived it in the 1990s. Black can accept and return the pawn later,
or decline with 4...Bb6.

## Ruy Lopez
Also: Spanish game; Spanish opening; 3. Bb5; Ruy
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5. Named after the Spanish priest Ruy López de
Segura, who wrote about it in 1561. The bishop attacks the knight that
defends e5; the threat is not immediate, but the pressure lasts all game.
It is one of the deepest openings in chess and a world championship staple.
White plans c3 and d4 for a strong centre, often with the knight travelling
Nb1-d2-f1-g3. Main answers: 3...a6 (the Morphy Defense), 3...Nf6 (the Berlin)
and 3...f5 (the Schliemann).

## Ruy Lopez: Morphy Defense
Also: 3...a6; Morphy defence; main line Ruy Lopez
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5 a6, named after Paul Morphy. Black asks the bishop
its intentions straight away. After 4. Ba4 Nf6 5. O-O Be7 6. Re1 b5 7. Bb3 d6
8. c3 O-O you reach the Closed Ruy Lopez, one of the richest structures in
chess. 4. Bxc6 is the Exchange Variation. Having ...b5 available means Black
can always break the pin, but White's bishop often ends up strong on b3 or c2,
aimed at the kingside.

## Ruy Lopez: Closed
Also: closed Spanish; Chigorin variation; Breyer; Zaitsev
The main line of the Ruy Lopez after 5...Be7 and ...b5, ...d6, ...O-O. Long,
manoeuvring games: White plays c3, h3 and d4 and slowly regroups the
queen's knight to g3; Black chooses a system such as the Chigorin (...Na5 and
...c5), the Breyer (...Nb8 and back to d7) or the Zaitsev (...Bb7 and ...Re8).
Plans: White often closes the centre with d5 and attacks on the kingside;
Black looks for queenside play with ...c5 and ...a5, or for ...f5 later.

## Ruy Lopez: Berlin Defense
Also: Berlin; Berlin wall; Berlin endgame; 3...Nf6
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5 Nf6. Kramnik used it to beat Kasparov in their
2000 world championship match, and it has been a top-level drawing weapon
ever since, hence "the Berlin Wall". The main line goes 4. O-O Nxe4 5. d4 Nd6
6. Bxc6 dxc6 7. dxe5 Nf5 8. Qxd8+ Kxd8: queens come off early, Black loses the
right to castle and has doubled pawns, but has the bishop pair and a very
solid position. White's plan is a kingside pawn majority.

## Ruy Lopez: Marshall Attack
Also: Marshall gambit; 8...d5
In the Closed Ruy Lopez, 7...O-O 8. c3 d5!? Black gives up a pawn for a
fierce kingside attack: after 9. exd5 Nxd5 10. Nxe5 Nxe5 11. Rxe5 Black
throws pieces at White's king with ...c6, ...Bd6, ...Qh4 and ...Bg4. Frank
Marshall first played it against Capablanca in 1918 and lost, but it is now
so well analysed that top White players often avoid it with 8. a4 or 8. h3.

## Ruy Lopez: Exchange Variation
Also: exchange Spanish; 4. Bxc6
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5 a6 4. Bxc6 dxc6. White gives up the bishop pair to
damage Black's pawns. Black's queenside majority is doubled and cannot make
a passed pawn on its own, so in a pure pawn endgame White's kingside majority
would win. Black gets the bishops and open lines in return. Fischer played it
with great success. White's plan: trade pieces, d4, aim for the endgame.

## Ruy Lopez: Schliemann Defense
Also: Schliemann; Jaenisch gambit; 3...f5
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5 f5. A sharp counter-gambit: Black strikes at e4 at
once, a King's Gambit with colours reversed and a tempo less. White's best
answer is usually 4. Nc3. Risky but full of traps, popular in club and
correspondence play.

## Scotch Game
Also: Scotch; 3. d4; Scotch gambit
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. d4. White opens the centre immediately. After 3...exd4
4. Nxd4 White has a central knight and free development; Black usually
answers with 4...Nf6 (Mieses line with 5. Nxc6 bxc6 6. e5) or 4...Bc5. The
Scotch Gambit, 4. Bc4 instead of retaking, offers a pawn for fast play.
Kasparov brought the Scotch back into top-level chess in the 1990s.

## Four Knights Game
Also: four knights; 3. Nc3 Nf6
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Nc3 Nf6. All four knights out on their best squares.
Solid and symmetrical; the main lines are the Spanish Four Knights (4. Bb5)
and the Scotch Four Knights (4. d4). Black's sharpest reply is the Rubinstein
counter-gambit 4. Bb5 Nd4. A sensible, low-risk choice that teaches classical
development.

## Petrov's Defense
Also: Petrov; Petroff; Russian game; 2...Nf6
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nf6. Black counterattacks e4 instead of defending e5. Do not
copy too long: 3. Nxe5 Nxe4? is a known mistake, because after 4. Qe2 the
natural 4...Nf6?? loses the queen to the discovered check 5. Nc6+. So Black
plays 3...d6 first and only then 4...Nxe4. Very solid and symmetrical; a favourite of players who
want a safe draw with Black, like Kramnik and Caruana. Named after the
19th-century Russian master Alexander Petrov.

## Philidor Defense
Also: Philidor; 2...d6
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 d6. Black defends e5 with a pawn. Solid but passive: the
dark-squared bishop is shut in behind d6. Black often aims for the Hanham
setup with ...Nd7, ...Be7 and ...c6. It is named after François-André
Danican Philidor, the great 18th-century player. Morphy's Opera Game started
from this opening.

## King's Gambit
Also: King's gambit; 2. f4
1. e4 e5 2. f4. The most famous romantic opening. White offers the f-pawn to
deflect Black's e-pawn, open the f-file and take the centre with d4. It was
the main battleground of the 1800s. Black can accept (2...exf4), decline
(2...Bc5) or counterattack (2...d5, the Falkbeer). Risky for both sides: White's
king is loosened. Still played by attacking players for surprise value.

## King's Gambit Accepted
Also: King's gambit accepted; 2...exf4
After 2...exf4 Black tries to keep the extra pawn, often with ...g5. White's
main ideas are 3. Nf3 to stop ...Qh4+, then pressure on f7 with Bc4, the
open f-file after castling, and a big centre with d4. Famous sub-lines
include the Kieseritzky (4. h4 g4 5. Ne5) and the Muzio Gambit, which gives
up a whole knight for attack.

## King's Gambit Declined
Also: Falkbeer countergambit; 2...Bc5; classical King's gambit declined
Black refuses the pawn. 2...Bc5 stops White from castling short easily by
pinning along the a7-g1 diagonal, while the Falkbeer Countergambit 2...d5
3. exd5 e4 hits back in the centre. Declining is a practical choice: it
avoids theory and leaves White's loosened king as a lasting target.

## Vienna Game
Also: Vienna; 2. Nc3
1. e4 e5 2. Nc3. White develops the queen's knight first, keeping the option
of f4 (the Vienna Gambit) or quiet play with Bc4 and d3. It is flexible and
sneaky: after 2...Nf6 3. Bc4 Nxe4 the Frankenstein-Dracula Variation gets
wild. A good choice for players who like King's Gambit ideas with more
preparation.

## Bishop's Opening
Also: bishop opening; 2. Bc4
1. e4 e5 2. Bc4. White aims at f7 at once and keeps the f-pawn free to
advance. It often transposes to the Italian, the Two Knights or the Vienna.
Larsen and others liked it as a way to avoid the Petrov.

## Center Game
Also: centre game; 2. d4 exd4 3. Qxd4
1. e4 e5 2. d4 exd4 3. Qxd4. White recaptures with the queen, which is then
hit by 3...Nc6, losing time. White often follows with Qe3, Nc3, Bd2 and
castles long for an attack. Considered harmless but playable; it goes
against the "don't bring your queen out early" rule on purpose.

## Danish Gambit
Also: Danish; 3. c3
1. e4 e5 2. d4 exd4 3. c3. White offers one or even two pawns (after 3...dxc3
4. Bc4 cxb2 5. Bxb2) for two bishops raking the board and a big lead in
development. Very dangerous for the unprepared; the calm answer 3...d5
returns the pawn and equalises.

## Ponziani Opening
Also: Ponziani; 3. c3
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. c3. White prepares d4 with a pawn so as to recapture
with a pawn and keep a full centre. An old opening, revived now and then as a
surprise. Black's best replies hit the centre at once with 3...Nf6 or 3...d5.

## Latvian Gambit
Also: Latvian; 2...f5
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 f5. A very risky counter-gambit for Black, the King's Gambit
reversed with a tempo less. White's best is usually 3. Nxe5. It is rarely
seen at master level, but it leads to wild positions that can catch out a
careless opponent.

## Elephant Gambit
Also: elephant gambit; 2...d5
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 d5. Black strikes at the centre at once, offering a pawn.
Objectively dubious; White does well with 3. exd5 and then simple
development. Played as a surprise weapon.

## Sicilian Defense
Also: Sicilian; 1...c5; the Sicilian
1. e4 c5. The most popular and most successful answer to 1. e4. Black fights
for the d4 square with a flank pawn, creating an unbalanced position. After
the main line 2. Nf3 and 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4, Black has traded a wing pawn for
a centre pawn and gets a half-open c-file and a central pawn majority; White
gets a lead in development, more space and attacking chances on the kingside.
Major branches: the Najdorf, Dragon, Scheveningen, Sveshnikov, Taimanov, Kan
and Classical. White can avoid the Open Sicilian with the Alapin (2. c3), the
Closed Sicilian (2. Nc3 and g3), the Rossolimo (3. Bb5) or the Smith-Morra
Gambit.

## Sicilian Defense: Najdorf Variation
Also: Najdorf; Sicilian Najdorf; 5...a6
1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 a6. Named after Miguel
Najdorf, and the favourite of Fischer and Kasparov. The quiet-looking 5...a6
keeps knights and bishops off b5 and prepares ...e5 or ...e6 and queenside
expansion with ...b5 and ...Bb7. White chooses among sharp systems: 6. Bg5,
the English Attack (Be3, f3, Qd2, O-O-O, g4) and the positional 6. Be2.
Often both sides castle on opposite wings and race to attack. Very sharp and
heavily analysed.

## Sicilian Defense: Dragon Variation
Also: Dragon; Sicilian Dragon; Yugoslav attack
1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 g6. Black fianchettoes the
bishop on g7, where it bites down the long diagonal; the pawn shape is said to
look like a dragon. White's most dangerous answer is the Yugoslav Attack:
Be3, f3, Qd2, castling long, then h4-h5 to open the h-file and trade the
dragon bishop with Bh6. Black attacks on the queenside along the c-file, often
with an exchange sacrifice ...Rxc3. A race where one tempo decides.

## Sicilian Defense: Accelerated Dragon
Also: accelerated dragon; Maroczy bind
1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 g6. Black fianchettoes before playing
...d6, so that ...d5 can come in one move and the Yugoslav Attack is less
effective. White's main positional answer is the Maróczy Bind: pawns on c4
and e4 that clamp the d5 square, leading to a slower, squeezing game.

## Sicilian Defense: Scheveningen Variation
Also: Scheveningen; small centre; Keres attack
Black sets up pawns on d6 and e6, a "small centre" that controls d5 and e5
and is hard to break. Kasparov played it in his matches against Karpov.
White's sharpest try is the Keres Attack, 6. g4, throwing the g-pawn at the
f6 knight. Typical Black plans: ...a6, ...Qc7, ...b5 on the queenside, and a
central break with ...d5 or ...e5 at the right moment.

## Sicilian Defense: Lasker-Pelikan Variation
Also: Sveshnikov; Sicilian Sveshnikov; Pelikan; 5...e5
1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nf6 5. Nc3 e5. Black accepts a hole on
d5 and a backward d-pawn in exchange for time and active pieces. After
6. Ndb5 d6 7. Bg5 a6 8. Na3 b5 Black often gets doubled f-pawns but the bishop
pair and play with ...f5. Evgeny Sveshnikov developed it into a top-level
weapon; Carlsen used it in his 2018 world championship match.

## Sicilian Defense: Taimanov Variation
Also: Taimanov; Paulsen; 4...e6 5...Nc6
1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 e6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 Nc6. A flexible setup: Black delays
...d6 and ...Nf6, keeping options for the pieces. Typical moves are ...Qc7,
...a6 and ...b5. Named after Mark Taimanov. Solid, rich in ideas, and less
theory-heavy than the Najdorf.

## Sicilian Defense: Kan Variation
Also: Kan; Paulsen; 4...a6
1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 e6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4 a6. Black keeps the most flexible pawn
setup possible, controls b5 and prepares ...Qc7, ...b5 and ...Bb7. White often
answers with c4, a Maróczy-style bind, leading to Hedgehog structures where
Black waits behind pawns on a6, b6, d6 and e6 for a well-timed ...b5 or ...d5.

## Sicilian Defense: Richter-Rauzer Variation
Also: Classical Sicilian; Richter-Rauzer; 6. Bg5
In the Classical Sicilian (2...d6 and 5...Nc6) White plays 6. Bg5, threatening
to take on f6 and double Black's pawns, then Qd2 and castles long. Black
often accepts doubled f-pawns for the bishop pair. Opposite-side castling and
mutual attacks are typical.

## Sicilian Defense: Alapin Variation
Also: Alapin; 2. c3; c3 Sicilian
1. e4 c5 2. c3. White prepares d4 so as to recapture with a pawn and keep a
classical centre, avoiding the huge theory of the Open Sicilian. Black's main
answers are 2...d5 (often with an isolated pawn structure after exchanges) and
2...Nf6, hitting e4. A solid, practical choice that tends to lead to calmer
positions.

## Sicilian Defense: Closed
Also: Closed Sicilian; 2. Nc3 with g3
White plays 2. Nc3 and a slow setup with g3, Bg2, d3 and often f4, aiming for
a kingside attack while keeping the centre closed. Spassky used it as a
weapon. Black usually mirrors with ...g6 and ...Bg7 and expands on the
queenside with ...Rb8 and ...b5.

## Sicilian Defense: Smith-Morra Gambit
Also: Smith-Morra; Morra gambit; 2. d4 cxd4 3. c3
1. e4 c5 2. d4 cxd4 3. c3. White gives up a pawn for rapid development and
open c- and d-files aimed at Black's queen and king. Popular at club level and
full of traps for both sides; in the Siberian Trap it is Black who wins, with
...Qc7, ...Ng4 and ...Nd4 threatening the queen and mate on h2 at once. Black
can accept with 3...dxc3, or decline with 3...Nf6 or 3...d5.

## Sicilian Defense: Nyezhmetdinov-Rossolimo Attack
Also: Rossolimo; 3. Bb5 Sicilian; Moscow variation
1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5 (or 2...d6 3. Bb5+, the Moscow Variation). White
develops quickly, castles and often takes on c6 to damage Black's pawns,
avoiding the Open Sicilian's sharp theory. A favourite of positional
players and a frequent top-level choice.

## Sicilian Defense: Grand Prix Attack
Also: Grand Prix; f4 Sicilian
White plays Nc3 and an early f4, then Nf3, Bc4 or Bb5, castles and attacks on
the kingside with f5 and Qe1-h4. Popular in British weekend tournaments in
the 1980s, hence the name. Black does well with quick counterplay in the
centre, such as ...d5.

## French Defense
Also: French; 1...e6; the French
1. e4 e6, usually followed by 2. d4 d5. Black challenges the centre with a
solid pawn chain. The drawback is the light-squared bishop on c8, shut in
behind the e6 pawn: the "French bishop". Black's typical plans are breaking
White's centre with ...c5 and ...f6. Main lines: the Winawer (3. Nc3 Bb4), the
Classical (3. Nc3 Nf6), the Tarrasch (3. Nd2), the Advance (3. e5) and the
Exchange (3. exd5). Solid and counterattacking; Botvinnik and Korchnoi were
famous French players.

## French Defense: Advance Variation
Also: Advance French; 3. e5
1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. e5. White grabs space and locks the centre. Black attacks
the base of the pawn chain at once with 3...c5, then ...Nc6, ...Qb6 and
...Nge7-f5, all aimed at d4. White defends d4 with c3, Nf3 and often Bd3 or
Be2, and later uses the space for a kingside attack. Nimzowitsch championed
it.

## French Defense: Winawer Variation
Also: Winawer; 3...Bb4
1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3 Bb4. Black pins the knight and, after 4. e5 c5 5. a3
Bxc3+ 6. bxc3, gives up the dark-squared bishop to damage White's pawns.
White gets the bishop pair and attacking chances on the dark squares,
especially with Qg4 hitting g7; Black gets a target on c3 and a solid
position. One of the sharpest and most complex lines of the French.

## French Defense: Classical Variation
Also: classical French; 3...Nf6; Steinitz variation; MacCutcheon
1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3 Nf6. After 4. Bg5 the game becomes sharp; 4. e5 Nfd7,
the Steinitz Variation, leads to positions where White supports the e5 pawn
with f4 and Nf3, and Black hits the chain with ...c5 and ...f6.

## French Defense: Tarrasch Variation
Also: Tarrasch French; 3. Nd2
1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. Nd2. White avoids the Winawer pin and keeps the c-pawn
free to support d4. Black often accepts an isolated queen's pawn after 3...c5
4. exd5 exd5, or plays 3...Nf6 for a closed position. A solid, positional
choice named after Siegbert Tarrasch.

## French Defense: Exchange Variation
Also: Exchange French; 3. exd5
1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5 3. exd5 exd5. The position becomes symmetrical, and Black's
light-squared bishop is freed. It has a drawish reputation, but there is play
for both sides if they castle on opposite wings or create imbalances. A safe
choice for players who dislike the French structures.

## Caro-Kann Defense
Also: Caro-Kann; Caro Kann; 1...c6
1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5. Black supports ...d5 with the c-pawn, so the light-squared
bishop can still come out (unlike the French). Very solid, with sound pawn
structures, and a reputation for being hard to beat: Capablanca, Botvinnik,
Petrosian, Karpov and Anand all played it. Named after Horatio Caro and
Marcus Kann, who analysed it in the 1880s. Main lines: the Classical
(3. Nc3 dxe4 4. Nxe4 Bf5), the Advance (3. e5), the Exchange and Panov Attack,
and the Two Knights.

## Caro-Kann Defense: Advance Variation
Also: Advance Caro-Kann; Caro-Kann Advance; 3. e5
1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. e5 Bf5. White gains space; Black develops the bishop
outside the pawn chain, then plays ...e6, ...c5 and ...Nc6 to attack d4. White
can play quietly with Nf3, Be2 and c3, or sharply with an early g4 and h4 to
chase the bishop. Modern main line, rich in ideas for both sides.

## Caro-Kann Defense: Classical Variation
Also: Classical Caro-Kann; 4...Bf5
1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. Nc3 dxe4 4. Nxe4 Bf5. Black develops the bishop with
tempo on the knight. White usually plays 5. Ng3 Bg6 6. h4 h6 7. Nf3 Nd7
8. h5 Bh7, gaining kingside space, then castles long. Black's position is
compact and solid, with a sound pawn structure for the endgame.

## Caro-Kann Defense: Panov Attack
Also: Panov; Panov-Botvinnik attack; exchange Caro-Kann with c4
1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5 3. exd5 cxd5 4. c4. White plays for an isolated queen's
pawn position with active pieces and kingside attacking chances; Black aims to
blockade d5 and win the isolated pawn in the long run. The quiet Exchange
Variation, 4. Bd3, keeps a symmetrical structure instead.

## Scandinavian Defense
Also: Scandinavian; centre counter; 1...d5
1. e4 d5. Black attacks e4 at once. After 2. exd5 Qxd5 3. Nc3 the queen moves
again, usually to a5, d6 or d8, losing time but reaching a solid, simple
position with a clear development plan: ...Nf6, ...Bf5 or ...Bg4, ...c6, ...e6.
2...Nf6, the Modern Variation, recaptures later with the knight. Carlsen has
played it at the top level.

## Alekhine Defense
Also: Alekhine; 1...Nf6
1. e4 Nf6. A hypermodern provocation: Black invites White's pawns forward with
2. e5 Nd5 3. d4 d6, intending to undermine the over-extended centre. White's
most ambitious answer is the Four Pawns Attack (c4, d4, e4 and f4); the calm
Modern Variation with 4. Nf3 is the main line. Alexander Alekhine introduced
it in 1921.

## Pirc Defense
Also: Pirc; 1...d6 2...Nf6 3...g6
1. e4 d6 2. d4 Nf6 3. Nc3 g6. Black lets White build a broad centre and then
attacks it from the side with the fianchettoed bishop and breaks such as
...c5 and ...e5. White's sharpest try is the Austrian Attack (f4), aiming for
e5; the 150 Attack (Be3, Qd2, Bh6) is a simple club favourite. Named after
Vasja Pirc.

## Modern Defense
Also: Modern; Robatsch; 1...g6
1. e4 g6. Even more flexible than the Pirc: Black fianchettoes first and
delays ...Nf6, keeping options such as ...c6 and ...d5, or ...a6 and ...b5. It
gives White a free hand in the centre, which Black then has to undermine.
Hypermodern in spirit.

## Nimzowitsch Defense
Also: Nimzowitsch defence; 1...Nc6
1. e4 Nc6. An offbeat defence from Aron Nimzowitsch. Black answers 2. d4 with
2...d5 or 2...e5, aiming for unusual positions. Rarely seen at the top, but a
playable surprise.

## Queen's Pawn Game
Also: 1. d4; queen pawn opening; closed games; d4 openings
1. d4 is the second most popular first move. The pawn on d4 is defended by the
queen, so White can build up more slowly than after 1. e4, and games tend to
be more strategic. Black's main answers are 1...d5 (the Queen's Gambit
openings) and 1...Nf6 (the Indian defences: King's Indian, Nimzo-Indian,
Queen's Indian, Grünfeld, Benoni), plus the Dutch (1...f5). White's main plan
is c4 to fight for d5; system players use the London or Colle instead.

## Queen's Gambit
Also: Queen's gambit; 2. c4; QG
1. d4 d5 2. c4. White offers the c-pawn to deflect Black's d-pawn from the
centre. It is not a true gambit: if Black takes, White usually wins the pawn
back. Black can accept (2...dxc4), decline with 2...e6 (Queen's Gambit
Declined) or 2...c6 (the Slav), or play the Albin or Chigorin. One of the most
respected openings in chess; the title of the 2020 Netflix series.

## Queen's Gambit Accepted
Also: QGA; 2...dxc4
1. d4 d5 2. c4 dxc4. Black takes the pawn but does not try to keep it.
Instead Black uses the time to strike at White's centre with ...c5 and
develop with ...Nf6, ...e6 and ...a6. White gets a free centre and often an
isolated queen's pawn after exchanges. Trying to hold the pawn with ...b5
tends to go wrong.

## Queen's Gambit Declined
Also: QGD; 2...e6; orthodox defence
1. d4 d5 2. c4 e6. Black keeps a strong point on d5 and develops with ...Nf6,
...Be7 and ...O-O. Very solid, played in world championships for over a
century. The drawback is the light-squared bishop on c8; Black's freeing
breaks are ...c5 and ...e5. Main systems: the Orthodox, the Tartakower, the
Lasker Defense and the Exchange Variation with its minority attack.

## Queen's Gambit Declined: Exchange Variation
Also: QGD exchange; Carlsbad structure; minority attack
White plays cxd5 exd5, creating the Carlsbad pawn structure. White's classic
plan is the minority attack: pushing the b-pawn to b5 to create a weak pawn
on c6 in Black's camp. Black looks for kingside play, the e4 square for a
knight, or ...Ne4 and ...f5. A model positional opening.

## Queen's Gambit Declined: Albin Countergambit
Also: Albin; Albin countergambit; Lasker trap; 2...e5
1. d4 d5 2. c4 e5. Black offers a pawn and after 3. dxe5 d4 gets a cramping
pawn on d4. It contains the Lasker Trap: 4. e3? Bb4+ 5. Bd2 dxe3! 6. Bxb4??
exf2+ 7. Ke2 fxg1=N+!, an underpromotion to a knight that wins. Objectively
dubious, but tricky.

## Queen's Gambit Declined: Chigorin Defense
Also: Chigorin; 2...Nc6
1. d4 d5 2. c4 Nc6. Black develops a piece against the classical rule of not
blocking the c-pawn, aiming for piece play and often giving up the bishop
pair for quick development. Named after Mikhail Chigorin; Morozevich played
it at the top level.

## Slav Defense
Also: Slav; 2...c6
1. d4 d5 2. c4 c6. Black supports d5 with the c-pawn, keeping the
light-squared bishop free to develop to f5 or g4. Very solid and one of the
most popular answers to the Queen's Gambit. In the main line Black takes on
c4 and develops the bishop with ...Bf5. A good choice for players who want
the Queen's Gambit Declined's solidity without the bad bishop.

## Semi-Slav Defense
Also: Semi-Slav; Meran; Botvinnik variation; Anti-Meran
Black combines ...c6 and ...e6 (for example 1. d4 d5 2. c4 c6 3. Nf3 Nf6
4. Nc3 e6). It looks quiet, but it leads to some of the sharpest lines in
chess: the Meran (with ...dxc4 and ...b5) and the Botvinnik Variation (5. Bg5
dxc4 6. e4 b5), where both sides create passed pawns and attacks on opposite
wings.

## Tarrasch Defense
Also: Tarrasch defence; 3...c5
1. d4 d5 2. c4 e6 3. Nc3 c5. Black accepts an isolated queen's pawn in return
for free, active piece play. Siegbert Tarrasch believed the activity was
worth the weakness. White usually fianchettoes with g3 and Bg2 to pressure
d5. Kasparov played it in his youth.

## King's Indian Defense
Also: King's Indian; KID; 1...Nf6 2...g6 with ...d6
1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7 4. e4 d6. Black lets White build a big centre,
then strikes back with ...e5 or ...c5. In the main lines White plays d5 and
attacks on the queenside with c5, while Black attacks on the kingside with
...Nh5 or ...Ne8, ...f5, ...f4 and ...g5: a race. Fischer and Kasparov made it
famous; it is a choice for players who want to play for a win with Black.
Main systems: Classical (Nf3, Be2), Sämisch (f3), Four Pawns (f4) and
Fianchetto (g3).

## King's Indian Defense: Orthodox Variation
Also: Classical King's Indian; Mar del Plata; 7. O-O Nc6 8. d5 Ne7
The main line of the King's Indian: 5. Nf3 O-O 6. Be2 e5 7. O-O Nc6 8. d5
Ne7. The centre is locked, so each side attacks where its pawns point: White
on the queenside with b4 and c5, Black on the kingside with ...Nd7 or ...Ne8,
...f5, ...f4 and ...g5. Sometimes called the Mar del Plata Variation after a
1953 game. Speed matters more than material.

## Nimzo-Indian Defense
Also: Nimzo-Indian; Nimzo; 3...Bb4
1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 e6 3. Nc3 Bb4. Black pins the knight to stop e4, and is ready
to give up the bishop for the knight to double White's c-pawns. One of the
most respected defences in chess, created by Aron Nimzowitsch. White's main
answers: 4. e3 (Rubinstein), 4. Qc2 (Classical, avoiding doubled pawns) and
4. a3 (Sämisch). Typical themes: bishop pair versus structure, control of e4.

## Queen's Indian Defense
Also: Queen's Indian; 3...b6
1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 e6 3. Nf3 b6. When White avoids the Nimzo-Indian pin with
3. Nf3, Black fianchettoes the queen's bishop to control e4 and the long
diagonal. Solid and positional. White often answers with 4. g3 or the
Petrosian System 4. a3.

## Bogo-Indian Defense
Also: Bogo-Indian; Bogo; 3...Bb4+
1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 e6 3. Nf3 Bb4+. A check instead of the Nimzo-Indian pin, after
White has played Nf3. Solid and simple; Black often trades the bishop for a
knight. Named after Efim Bogoljubov.

## Grünfeld Defense
Also: Grünfeld; Grunfeld; 3...d5
1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 d5. Black allows White a big pawn centre after
4. cxd5 Nxd5 5. e4 Nxc3 6. bxc3, then attacks it with ...Bg7, ...c5 and ...Nc6.
A dynamic, hypermodern defence named after Ernst Grünfeld (1922); Fischer and
Kasparov played it in world championships. White's plans: hold the centre,
then push d5, or attack with h4.

## Benoni Defense
Also: Benoni; Modern Benoni; 2...c5
1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 c5 3. d5 e6 4. Nc3 exd5 5. cxd5 d6. Black gets a queenside
pawn majority and a fianchettoed bishop, while White has a central majority
and plans e4 and e5. Sharp and imbalanced; White must watch the queenside,
Black the central break. Tal used it to great effect.

## Benko Gambit
Also: Benko; Volga gambit; 3...b5
1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 c5 3. d5 b5. Black gives up a pawn for long-term queenside
pressure: after 4. cxb5 a6 5. bxa6 Bxa6 Black's rooks use the open a- and
b-files, and the bishop on g7 bears down the long diagonal. The compensation
often lasts into the endgame. Named after Pal Benko.

## Dutch Defense
Also: Dutch; 1...f5; Stonewall; Leningrad Dutch
1. d4 f5. Black fights for e4 with the f-pawn and aims for kingside attacks.
Three main setups: the Stonewall (pawns on c6, d5, e6, f5, very solid but with
a hole on e5), the Leningrad (with ...g6 and ...Bg7, King's Indian style) and
the Classical (...e6, ...Be7, ...d6). The early ...f5 loosens Black's king, so
lines like the Staunton Gambit (2. e4) are dangerous.

## Catalan Opening
Also: Catalan; 3. g3
1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 e6 3. g3 d5 4. Bg2. White combines the Queen's Gambit with a
fianchettoed bishop that presses on the long diagonal toward b7 and a8. White
often gives up the c4 pawn for a while to get long-term queenside pressure.
Named after Catalonia, where it was introduced in a 1929 Barcelona
tournament. A favourite of Kramnik and Carlsen.

## London System
Also: London; Jobava London; Bf4 system
A system for White built on d4 and an early Bf4, followed by e3, Nf3, c3,
Nbd2 and Bd3, played against almost anything. White builds a solid pyramid and plays the same plans every game:
the bishop sits outside the pawn chain, the knight goes to e5, and White
often attacks on the kingside. Low on theory, so very popular with club
players; Carlsen has used it too. Black's best tries hit d4 early with ...c5.

## Colle System
Also: Colle; Colle-Zukertort
A system for White: d4, Nf3, e3, Bd3, c3, Nbd2 and O-O, then e4 to open the
centre. The bishop
on d3 points at Black's king. The Colle-Zukertort setup plays b3 and Bb2
instead of c3. Simple to learn and good at club level; criticised for being
passive because the dark-squared bishop sits behind e3.

## Torre Attack
Also: Torre; 3. Bg5
1. d4 Nf6 2. Nf3 e6 3. Bg5 (or 2...g6 3. Bg5). White pins the knight and builds
with e3, c3 and Nbd2. A system opening named after Carlos Torre, who beat
Lasker with it in 1925. Solid, with sneaky attacking chances.

## Trompowsky Attack
Also: Trompowsky; Tromp; 2. Bg5
1. d4 Nf6 2. Bg5. White threatens to double Black's pawns with Bxf6 at once.
Black can answer 2...Ne4, 2...e6 or 2...d5. Offbeat but respectable, used by
grandmasters to avoid mainstream theory. Named after Octávio Trompowsky.

## Blackmar-Diemer Gambit
Also: BDG; Blackmar Diemer; 1. d4 d5 2. e4
1. d4 d5 2. e4 dxe4 3. Nc3 Nf6 4. f3. White offers a pawn for a lead in
development, an open f-file and attacking chances. Objectively dubious, but a
cult favourite of attacking club players.

## Indian Defense: Budapest Gambit
Also: Budapest; Budapest gambit; 2...e5
1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 e5. Black offers a pawn and usually wins it back, attacking
e5 with ...Ng4, ...Nc6 and ...Qe7. Contains tricky traps; objectively
slightly worse for Black but playable.

## Old Indian Defense
Also: Old Indian; 1...Nf6 2...d6
1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 d6. Like the King's Indian, but the bishop goes to e7 instead
of g7. Solid and cramped, Black aiming for ...e5.

## Englund Gambit
Also: Englund; 1...e5 against d4; Englund trap
1. d4 e5. A dubious gambit known for the Englund Trap: 2. dxe5 Nc6 3. Nf3 Qe7
4. Bf4? Qb4+ 5. Bd2 Qxb2 6. Bc3?? Bb4!, and Black wins material, with 7. Qd2
Bxc3 8. Qxc3 Qc1 mate. Good against beginners, bad against correct play.

## English Opening
Also: English; 1. c4
1. c4. White controls d5 from the flank and keeps central pawns flexible. It
often transposes to 1. d4 openings, or plays like a Sicilian with colours
reversed after 1...e5. Main branches: the Symmetrical (1...c5), the Reversed
Sicilian (1...e5) and the Anglo-Indian (1...Nf6). Popular with positional
players such as Botvinnik, Kramnik and Carlsen.

## English Opening: Symmetrical Variation
Also: Symmetrical English; 1...c5
1. c4 c5. Black copies White, often with both sides fianchettoing. Positional
and manoeuvring; plans revolve around the d4 and d5 squares and queenside
expansion with b4 or ...b5. The Hedgehog setup, Black with pawns on a6, b6,
d6 and e6, comes from here.

## English Opening: King's English Variation
Also: reversed Sicilian; King's English; 1...e5
1. c4 e5. A Sicilian Defence with colours reversed and an extra tempo for
White. White usually plays Nc3, g3 and Bg2, controlling d5 and the long
diagonal; Black occupies the centre with ...Nc6, ...g6 or ...Nf6 and ...d6.

## Réti Opening
Also: Réti; Reti; 1. Nf3 2. c4
1. Nf3 d5 2. c4. A hypermodern opening by Richard Réti: White controls the
centre with pieces and flank pawns, often fianchettoing one or both bishops.
It can transpose into the English, the Catalan or Queen's Gambit lines.
Flexible, positional, and good for avoiding theory.

## Zukertort Opening
Also: 1. Nf3; Zukertort
1. Nf3. A flexible first move that develops a piece, controls e5 and d4, and
keeps options for White's pawns. It often transposes to 1. d4 openings, the
English, the Réti or the King's Indian Attack. Named after Johannes Zukertort.

## King's Indian Attack
Also: KIA; King's Indian attack
White plays Nf3, g3, Bg2, O-O, d3, Nbd2 and e4, a King's Indian Defence with
colours reversed. Often used against the French or the Sicilian. White's
classic plan: push e5, then attack the kingside with h4, Nf1-h2-g4 and Bh6.
Fischer used it in the 1960s. Easy to learn because the setup is always the
same.

## Bird Opening
Also: Bird; 1. f4; From's gambit
1. f4. White controls e5 and aims for a Dutch Defence with colours reversed.
The sharp reply 1...e5, From's Gambit, sacrifices a pawn to attack White's
loosened king. Named after Henry Bird. Rare, but playable.

## Nimzo-Larsen Attack
Also: Larsen; 1. b3; Nimzo-Larsen
1. b3. White fianchettoes the queen's bishop at once to control the long
diagonal and e5. Bent Larsen played it at the top level in the 1960s and 70s.
Flexible and offbeat.

## Hungarian Opening
Also: 1. g3; Benko opening; Hungarian
1. g3. White prepares Bg2 and keeps every central option. It usually
transposes into the English, the Catalan or the King's Indian Attack.

## Polish Opening
Also: Sokolsky; orangutan; 1. b4
1. b4. White grabs queenside space and prepares Bb2. The pawn on b4 is a
target, so Black often answers 1...e5 hitting it with the bishop. Rare, but
Tartakower once played it after visiting the zoo, hence the nickname "the
Orangutan".

## Grob Opening
Also: Grob; 1. g4
1. g4. An eccentric first move that weakens White's kingside. The best answer
is a central reply such as 1...d5, hitting the bishop that will come to g2.
Mostly a speed-chess surprise.

## Bongcloud Attack
Also: Bongcloud; 1. e4 e5 2. Ke2
1. e4 e5 2. Ke2. A joke opening: the king walks out on move two, blocking its
own bishop and queen and losing the right to castle. It became famous online
when Carlsen and Nakamura played it in blitz, and in 2021 they both played it
in a serious online event, leading to a quick draw by repetition.
