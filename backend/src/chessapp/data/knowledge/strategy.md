# Strategy

Notes on strategy and positional play: opening principles, pawn structure,
the pieces, planning, and how to think during a game. Written for this app in
its own words.

Sources the facts were checked against: Wikipedia's articles on chess
strategy, pawn structure and the individual concepts; Nimzowitsch, My System;
Silman, How to Reassess Your Chess; chess.com and Lichess study material on
the opening principles.

## Opening principles
Also: how to play the opening; opening rules; what to do in the opening; development rules
The classic opening guidelines: fight for the centre with pawns and pieces;
develop knights and bishops quickly, usually knights before bishops; castle
early to get the king safe; do not move the same piece twice without a
reason; do not bring the queen out early, where it can be chased with tempo;
connect your rooks; and do not grab pawns at the cost of development. They
are guidelines, not laws: strong players break them when the position
justifies it.

## The centre
Also: center; control the center; central squares; why is the center important
The four central squares are d4, e4, d5 and e5. Pieces placed in or aimed at
the centre control more squares and can switch quickly to either wing. A
player who controls the centre has more space and can attack where it suits
them. Classical play occupies the centre with pawns; hypermodern play controls
it from a distance with pieces and fianchettoed bishops, and attacks the
opponent's pawn centre later.

## Development
Also: develop your pieces; developing; lead in development; piece development
Development means bringing pieces off the back rank to active squares. A lead
in development, more pieces in play than the opponent, is a temporary
advantage: it is used by opening the position and attacking before the
opponent catches up. In closed positions it matters less. Each tempo spent on
a pawn grab or a queen sortie in the opening is a tempo not spent developing.

## King safety
Also: castle early; king safety; why castle; keep your king safe
The king is the piece the whole game is about, so it usually needs shelter in
the opening and middlegame. Castling moves it away from the centre, where
files tend to open, and brings a rook toward the middle. Keep the pawns in
front of the castled king intact where possible: each pawn move there can
leave weak squares. In the endgame, with few pieces left, the king becomes an
active, fighting piece.

## Castling strategy
Also: when to castle; castle kingside or queenside; opposite side castling; same side castling
Kingside castling is quicker and usually safer, since the king ends on g1 or
g8 behind three pawns. Queenside castling takes longer and leaves the a-pawn
unguarded, but puts the rook straight onto the d-file. When players castle on
opposite sides, both can throw pawns at the enemy king without weakening
their own, so the game becomes a race; when they castle on the same side,
play is usually slower and more positional.

## King in the centre
Also: uncastled king; king stuck in the center; lost castling rights
A king left in the centre when the position opens is often in serious danger:
the central files open quickly and the rooks stay disconnected. Sacrifices
that open lines against an uncastled king, for example on e6 or f7, are a
classic attacking theme. If you cannot castle, sometimes the king can walk to
safety by hand, a manoeuvre called artificial castling.

## Material values
Also: piece values; how much is a piece worth; point values; pawn is worth
The usual rough values are pawn 1, knight 3, bishop 3, rook 5 and queen 9; the
king is priceless. A bishop is often worth slightly more than a knight, the
bishop pair noticeably more. Two minor pieces usually beat a rook and pawn in
the middlegame, and a rook and two pawns are about equal to two minor pieces.
Values change with the position: activity, pawn structure and king safety can
outweigh a pawn or more.

## The exchange
Also: winning the exchange; exchange sacrifice; rook for a minor piece
"The exchange" is the difference between a rook and a minor piece. Winning the
exchange means getting a rook for a bishop or knight. An exchange sacrifice
gives a rook for a minor piece on purpose, to destroy a pawn structure, kill
an important defender or get a dominant minor piece. Petrosian was famous for
positional exchange sacrifices, and ...Rxc3 is a classic theme in the
Sicilian Dragon.

## When to trade pieces
Also: exchanging pieces; trading pieces; should I trade; simplify
Trade pieces when you are ahead in material, since fewer pieces make the extra
material count for more; when you are being attacked; when the trade removes
the opponent's best piece or leaves them with a bad one; and when the
resulting endgame suits you. Avoid trades when you are attacking, when you
have more space (the cramped side wants trades), or when you are behind in
material.

## Space
Also: space advantage; cramped position; more space
Having more space means your pawns control more of the board, so your pieces
have more room to manoeuvre and switch from wing to wing. The cramped side
suffers from pieces getting in each other's way and should look for trades
and pawn breaks to free the position. Too much space can also be a weakness
if the advanced pawns cannot be supported.

## Initiative
Also: having the initiative; tempo; momentum; forcing moves
The initiative belongs to the player who is making threats and dictating
play, forcing the opponent to respond. It is a dynamic advantage: it must be
used, often by attacking or opening lines, before the opponent consolidates.
Giving up a pawn for a lasting initiative is often a good trade.

## Tempo
Also: tempi; gain a tempo; lose a tempo; time in chess
A tempo is one move, used as a unit of time. You gain a tempo by developing
with a threat, so the opponent has to spend a move answering; you lose one
by moving a piece twice or by being chased around. In sharp positions, and
in races between attacks on opposite wings, a single tempo can decide the
game.

## Pawn structure
Also: pawn structures; pawns are the soul of chess; pawn skeleton
Philidor wrote that pawns are the soul of chess. Pawns cannot move backward,
so every pawn move is a permanent decision, and the pawn structure decides
where the pieces belong and which plans make sense. Typical structures
include the isolated queen's pawn, the Carlsbad, hanging pawns, the Maróczy
bind and the pawn chains of the French and King's Indian. Learning these
structures is one of the fastest ways to improve.

## Isolated queen's pawn
Also: IQP; isolani; isolated pawn; isolated d-pawn
A pawn with no friendly pawns on the files next to it is isolated; it cannot
be protected by another pawn, and the square in front of it is a perfect
outpost for an enemy piece. An isolated d-pawn (IQP) gives its owner space
and open lines for active pieces, often with a kingside attack and the break
d5. The defender blockades the square in front, trades pieces, and aims for
an endgame where the pawn is weak. Nimzowitsch: first restrain, then
blockade, then destroy.

## Doubled pawns
Also: doubled pawns; double pawns
Two pawns of the same colour on one file. They are usually a weakness: they
cannot protect each other, they are hard to advance, and a majority with
doubled pawns may not create a passed pawn. They can also bring benefits: an
open file for a rook, and extra control of central squares. Many openings,
such as the Nimzo-Indian, are built around accepting doubled pawns for
something else.

## Backward pawn
Also: backward pawn; weak pawn
A pawn that has fallen behind its neighbours and cannot be supported by them,
so it cannot advance safely. It is often on a half-open file, where enemy
rooks attack it, and the square in front of it is a hole for an enemy piece.
The backward d6 pawn in the Sicilian Sveshnikov and Najdorf is a famous
example, kept healthy by Black's active pieces.

## Passed pawn
Also: passed pawns; passer; create a passed pawn; push passed pawns
A pawn with no enemy pawns in front of it on its own file or the files next
to it, so no pawn can stop it from promoting. Passed pawns are strong in the
endgame and tie down enemy pieces. "Passed pawns must be pushed," said
Nimzowitsch, and the best piece to block one is a knight. A protected passed
pawn is defended by another pawn; an outside passed pawn is far from the
other pawns and lures the enemy king away.

## Connected passed pawns
Also: connected passers; two connected passed pawns
Two passed pawns on neighbouring files protect each other as they advance and
are very hard to stop. Connected passed pawns on the sixth rank usually beat
a rook on its own. They are one of the strongest assets in an endgame.

## Pawn chains
Also: pawn chain; attack the base of the pawn chain
A diagonal line of pawns protecting each other, as in the French Defence (d4
and e5 against d5 and e6) or the King's Indian. The rule, from Nimzowitsch,
is to attack the base of the chain, the pawn at the back, since it is not
protected by a pawn. Each side usually attacks on the wing where its pawn
chain points.

## Hanging pawns
Also: hanging pawns; c and d pawns
Two pawns side by side, usually on c4 and d4 or c5 and d5, with no friendly
pawns on the files next to them. They control important squares and can
support attacks, but they can also become targets: if one advances, the other
may become backward or isolated. Who benefits depends on piece activity.

## Pawn majority
Also: queenside majority; kingside majority; majority
A pawn majority is having more pawns than the opponent on one side of the
board. A healthy majority can create a passed pawn. A queenside majority is
often valuable in the endgame because it produces a passed pawn far from the
kings.

## Minority attack
Also: minority attack; b4-b5
A plan where fewer pawns attack a larger group to create weaknesses in it.
The classic case is the Queen's Gambit Declined Exchange Variation: White
pushes the b-pawn to b5 against Black's a-, b- and c-pawns to leave Black
with a weak, isolated or backward c-pawn.

## Pawn islands
Also: pawn islands; fewer pawn islands
A pawn island is a group of pawns separated from other groups by files with
no friendly pawns. Capablanca's guideline: the side with fewer pawn islands
is usually better off, because each island needs protecting.

## Weak squares
Also: holes; weak square; outpost square
A weak square, or hole, is one that can no longer be attacked by a pawn.
Enemy pieces, especially knights, can settle there safely. Pawn moves create
holes, so every pawn move should be checked for what it gives up. Weak
squares of one colour, a weak colour complex, appear when the bishop of that
colour is gone.

## Outposts
Also: outpost; knight outpost; strong square
An outpost is a square, usually in enemy territory, that cannot be attacked
by an enemy pawn and is supported by a friendly pawn. A knight on an outpost
on the fifth or sixth rank can dominate the game. Creating an outpost and
bringing a knight to it is a classic positional plan.

## Knights
Also: knight; how to use knights; knight on the rim is dim
Knights are short-range pieces that jump and so are not blocked by pawns.
They are best in closed positions and on central outposts, and worst on the
edge: "a knight on the rim is dim", since it controls half as many squares.
A knight in the centre controls eight squares. Knights are good blockaders of
passed pawns and good at forks.

## Bishops
Also: bishop; long diagonal; how to use bishops
Bishops are long-range pieces that stay on one colour all game. They are best
in open positions with pawns off the long diagonals, and most powerful in
pairs. A fianchettoed bishop on g2 or b2 controls the long diagonal. A bishop
does well in endgames with pawns on both wings, where its range counts.

## The bishop pair
Also: two bishops; bishop pair advantage; why is the bishop pair good
Having both bishops while the opponent has bishop and knight, or two knights,
is a real advantage, worth roughly half a pawn. Together the bishops cover
squares of both colours, so no square is safe from them, and they grow
stronger as the position opens and in the endgame. The side without the pair
tries to keep the position closed and the pawns fixed.

## Good and bad bishops
Also: bad bishop; good bishop; French bishop
A bishop is "bad" when its own pawns are fixed on squares of its colour,
blocking it, and "good" when the pawns sit on the other colour, leaving it
free. A bad bishop can still be a useful defender of those pawns. The
light-squared bishop in the French Defence is the textbook bad bishop. As a
rule, put your pawns on the opposite colour to your remaining bishop.

## Bishop versus knight
Also: bishop or knight; which is better bishop or knight; knight vs bishop
Neither is better in general. Bishops prefer open positions, pawns on both
wings and long diagonals; knights prefer closed positions, fixed pawns and
outposts. In endgames with pawns on only one side, a knight is usually fine;
with pawns on both sides, the bishop is usually better.

## Rooks
Also: rook; how to use rooks; rook activity
Rooks need open lines. Put them on open files (no pawns) or half-open files
(only enemy pawns), double them, and use them to invade the seventh rank. In
the opening rooks are the last pieces to join; connecting the rooks, so they
see each other along the back rank, is a sign development is complete.

## Open files
Also: open file; half-open file; rook on an open file
An open file has no pawns on it; a half-open file has only the opponent's
pawns. Rooks and queens use these files to enter the enemy position. Control
of the only open file is often decisive. Doubling rooks on a file
strengthens control.

## Rook on the seventh
Also: seventh rank; rook on the 7th; pig on the seventh
A rook on the seventh rank (the second rank for Black) attacks pawns still on
their starting squares and traps the enemy king on the back rank. Two rooks
on the seventh, sometimes called "pigs", can be devastating, often forcing
perpetual check or mate.

## The queen
Also: queen; using the queen; queen early
The queen is the strongest piece, combining rook and bishop. Brought out too
early it gets chased by minor pieces, losing time. In the middlegame it is
often best a little behind the action, supporting attacks. A queen is good at
double attacks and at exploiting a weakened king.

## Piece activity
Also: active pieces; improve your worst piece; passive pieces; activity
Active pieces control many squares and take part in the play; passive pieces
defend or are blocked. A good habit when there is no direct plan is to find
your worst piece and improve it. Activity often matters more than a pawn:
giving up material to activate pieces is a common good decision.

## Centralization
Also: centralization; centralize; centralise
Pieces in the centre control more squares and can reach either wing quickly.
A centralized queen or knight is often a sign of a healthy position, and in
the endgame the king should head for the centre.

## Fianchetto
Also: fianchetto; fianchettoed bishop
Developing a bishop on the long diagonal by moving the knight's pawn one
square, for example g3 and Bg2. The bishop controls the long diagonal and the
centre from a distance, and helps protect the castled king. If the
fianchettoed bishop is traded, the squares around the king can become weak.
Used in the King's Indian, Dragon, Catalan and many other systems.

## Hypermodernism
Also: hypermodern; hypermodern openings; control the centre from a distance
A school of thought from the 1920s, led by Nimzowitsch, Réti and Grünfeld. Its
idea: the centre need not be occupied by pawns at once; it can be controlled
from a distance by pieces, especially fianchettoed bishops, and the
opponent's pawn centre can then be attacked and undermined. Openings like the
King's Indian, Grünfeld, Alekhine, Nimzo-Indian and Réti come from it.

## Prophylaxis
Also: prophylaxis; prophylactic move; stop the opponent's plan
Prophylaxis means preventing the opponent's plans before they happen, rather
than only pursuing your own. Ask "what does my opponent want to do?" and take
it away. Nimzowitsch made it a concept, and Petrosian and Karpov were masters
of it. Making luft for your king, or moving it off a dangerous diagonal in
advance, are simple examples.

## Blockade
Also: blockade; blockading a passed pawn
Placing a piece directly in front of an enemy pawn, especially a passed pawn,
to stop it moving. The knight is the ideal blockader, because it keeps its
activity while blocking. Nimzowitsch made it central to his system.

## Overprotection
Also: overprotection; overprotect
Nimzowitsch's idea of defending an important point, such as a strong central
pawn, more times than it is attacked. The defending pieces gain from it: they
are well placed, and the point holds no matter what.

## Opposite-coloured bishops in the middlegame
Also: opposite colored bishops attack; bishops of opposite colours middlegame
With bishops of opposite colours, the attacker effectively has an extra
piece, because the defender's bishop cannot cover the squares the attacker's
bishop hits. So in the middlegame they favour the attacking side. In the
endgame the opposite is true: they are famously drawish.

## Weak back rank
Also: back rank weakness; luft; escape square for the king
A king castled behind three unmoved pawns has no escape square, so a rook or
queen check on the back rank can be mate. Making "luft" (German for air) by
moving one pawn, often h3 or h6, gives the king a square and removes the
danger. Back-rank threats decide a great many games at every level.

## Attacking the king
Also: how to attack; kingside attack; attack the king; mating attack
A successful attack usually needs more attackers than defenders near the
king, open lines (files or diagonals) toward it, and a weakness in its pawn
cover. Typical methods: pawn storms when the kings are on opposite sides,
sacrifices to break the pawn shield, bringing the queen and knights close,
and removing key defenders such as the knight on f6. An attack that fails
usually leaves weaknesses behind, so commit when the conditions are there.

## Pawn breaks
Also: pawn break; break; lever
A pawn break is a pawn move that challenges an enemy pawn, opening lines or
changing the structure, such as ...c5 against White's d4 in the French, or f4
in the King's Indian. In most closed positions, finding and preparing the
right pawn break is the main plan. Prepare it with your pieces, then strike.

## Planning
Also: how to make a plan; what plan; I don't know what to do; middlegame plan
A plan comes from the position's features: weaknesses in both camps, the
pawn structure, which pieces are good and bad, open files and where each king
is. Decide where to play (the wing where your pawns point, or where the
opponent is weak), improve your worst piece, and prepare a pawn break. A
modest plan carried out is better than no plan.

## Candidate moves and calculation
Also: how to calculate; candidate moves; thinking process; how to think in chess
A practical thinking process: first ask what the opponent's last move
threatens; then list the candidate moves, starting with forcing moves (checks,
captures, threats); calculate each one far enough to reach a clear position;
compare the results, and before moving do a quick blunder check. Kotov's book
Think Like a Grandmaster made the idea of candidate moves famous.

## Blunder check
Also: avoid blunders; hanging pieces; why do I keep blundering; stop blundering
Most games between club players are decided by pieces left undefended or by
missed tactics. Before each move ask: what does my opponent threaten? Which
of my pieces are undefended? After my move, what checks, captures and threats
can my opponent play? "Loose pieces drop off" (LPDO, John Nunn) is a good
reminder to keep pieces protected.

## Converting an advantage
Also: how to win a won game; converting; winning technique; when you are winning
When ahead in material, trade pieces (not pawns), keep the king safe,
eliminate the opponent's counterplay before pressing, and avoid unnecessary
risks. Simplifying into a clearly won endgame is often the cleanest way. The
"principle of two weaknesses": one weakness can be defended, so create a
second one on the other side of the board to stretch the defence.

## Defending a worse position
Also: how to defend; defending; losing position; swindle
When worse, make the opponent's task as hard as possible: look for active
counterplay, keep pieces on if you need tricks or trade into a drawable
endgame (opposite-coloured bishops, rook endings), aim for a fortress, and
use the clock. Many lost positions are saved because the winning side
relaxed.

## Compensation
Also: compensation for material; dynamic compensation
Compensation is what a player gets for material given up: development, the
initiative, an attack, a strong pawn centre, a passed pawn, or the
opponent's weak king. Compensation is dynamic and often temporary, so the
side with it must act before it fades.

## Static and dynamic factors
Also: static advantage; dynamic advantage; long term versus short term
Static advantages last: better pawn structure, extra material, a good bishop
against a bad one, a weak enemy square. Dynamic advantages are temporary: a
lead in development, the initiative, an attack. A player with a dynamic edge
should hurry; a player with a static edge can be patient.

## Time management
Also: clock management; time trouble; zeitnot; using your time
Spend time on the critical moments, such as the transition out of the
opening, big decisions about pawn structure, and forcing sequences, and play
faster in simple positions. Time trouble (Zeitnot in German) causes many
blunders. In fast games, a simple solid move often beats a slow perfect one.

## How to improve at chess
Also: how to get better; improve; study plan; training; how to study chess
Most players improve fastest by solving tactics puzzles regularly, playing
longer games and reviewing them afterwards (ideally before using an engine),
learning basic endgames, and understanding the ideas of a small opening
repertoire rather than memorising lines. Studying annotated master games
shows how plans unfold.

## Choosing an opening
Also: best opening for beginners; which opening should I play; repertoire; what opening to learn
For beginners, openings that follow the classical rules work best: 1. e4 e5
with the Italian Game or the Scotch, or 1. d4 with the Queen's Gambit or the
London System. Pick openings whose middlegames you enjoy: tactical players
like the Sicilian or the King's Gambit, positional players the Caro-Kann or
the Queen's Gambit Declined. Understand the ideas before memorising moves,
learn the typical pawn structures, and keep the repertoire small at first.

## Gambits
Also: gambit; what is a gambit; pawn sacrifice in the opening
A gambit is an opening in which a player gives up material, usually a pawn,
for something else: a lead in development, open lines, the centre, or an
attack. Famous gambits include the King's Gambit, the Evans Gambit, the
Smith-Morra, the Benko and the Danish. The Queen's Gambit is a gambit in name
only, since White usually wins the pawn back. Accepting a gambit and then
returning the material at the right moment is a common way to neutralise it.

## Transpositions
Also: transposition; move order; transpose
A transposition is when a game reaches a known position by a different move
order. 1. Nf3 d5 2. d4 is a Queen's Pawn Game; 1. c4 e6 2. d4 d5 is a Queen's
Gambit Declined. Strong players use move orders to avoid an opponent's
favourite lines or to steer toward their own. Openings are named by position,
not by the order the moves came in.
