# Tactics

Notes on tactics, mating patterns and well-known opening traps. Written for
this app in its own words.

Sources the facts were checked against: Wikipedia's articles on chess
tactics, checkmate patterns and the individual traps; Renaud and Kahn, The
Art of the Checkmate; chess.com and Lichess puzzle-theme descriptions.

## Tactics
Also: what are tactics; tactics vs strategy; combination; chess is 99 percent tactics
Tactics are short, forcing sequences that win material or deliver mate,
built from motifs like forks, pins, skewers and discovered attacks. Strategy
is the long-term planning that creates the conditions for them. Richard
Teichmann's saying "chess is 99 percent tactics" exaggerates, but at club
level most games are decided tactically. A combination is a forcing sequence,
often with a sacrifice, that achieves a concrete goal.

## Fork
Also: double attack; forking; family fork; royal fork
One piece attacks two or more enemy pieces at once, and the opponent can only
save one. Knights are the classic forking piece because they attack squares
of the other colour and cannot be blocked; pawns and queens fork often too. A
knight fork of king and queen is a royal fork; one hitting king, queen and
rook is a family fork. Look for forks on squares the enemy pieces all share.

## Pin
Also: pin; pinned piece; absolute pin; relative pin
A piece cannot move without exposing a more valuable piece behind it to a
line attack by a bishop, rook or queen. In an absolute pin the piece behind is
the king, so the pinned piece may not legally move at all. In a relative pin
it can move, but loses what is behind it. Attack a pinned piece again, with a
pawn if possible: it cannot run away. Breaking a pin means moving the piece
behind, blocking, or attacking the pinning piece.

## Skewer
Also: skewer; x-ray attack
The reverse of a pin: a line piece attacks a valuable piece, which must move,
exposing a less valuable piece behind it to capture. Typical skewers are a
rook or bishop checking the king and winning the queen or rook behind it.
They are especially common in endgames with queens and rooks.

## Discovered attack
Also: discovered attack; discovery; unmask
Moving one piece uncovers an attack by another piece behind it, a bishop,
rook or queen. The moving piece can make its own threat at the same time,
so two threats arrive in one move. It is one of the most powerful tactical
motifs.

## Discovered check
Also: discovered check
A discovered attack where the uncovered piece gives check. The piece that
moves can then go almost anywhere, even capture the queen, because the
opponent must deal with the check first.

## Double check
Also: double check
Two pieces give check at once, usually through a discovered check where the
moving piece checks too. The only answer is a king move, since no single move
can block or capture both checkers. Double checks are behind many famous
mating attacks, including Morphy's Opera Game.

## Removing the defender
Also: removing the guard; undermining; eliminate the defender
Capturing or chasing away the piece that defends something, so the
undefended piece or square can then be taken or used. Often involves
exchanging off a key defender, such as the knight on f6 that guards h7.

## Deflection
Also: deflection; deflect
Forcing an enemy piece away from a square or line where it does an
important job, typically by offering it something to capture or by checking.
Once it has gone, the job it was doing is left undone, often allowing mate
or winning material.

## Decoy
Also: decoy; attraction; lure
Forcing an enemy piece, usually the king or queen, onto a particular square
where it becomes vulnerable to a fork, pin or mate. Often done with a
sacrifice on that square.

## Overloading
Also: overloaded piece; overload
A defending piece has more than one job, such as guarding two pieces or a
piece and a mating square. Attacking one of the things it guards forces it to
give up the other.

## Interference
Also: interference; cut off
Placing a piece on a line between an enemy piece and the square or piece it
defends, cutting the connection. Often the interfering piece is offered as a
sacrifice that cannot be taken safely.

## Clearance
Also: clearance sacrifice; clear a square; clear a line
Moving or sacrificing a piece to vacate a square or line so another piece can
use it, for example clearing a square for a knight fork or a diagonal for a
queen mate.

## Zwischenzug
Also: zwischenzug; in-between move; intermezzo
German for "in-between move". Instead of making the expected move, such as a
recapture, a player first plays a forcing move like a check or a bigger
threat, and only then recaptures, ending up better off. Checking for
zwischenzugs before assuming a recapture is forced avoids many surprises.

## X-ray
Also: x-ray; x-ray defense
A line piece acts through another piece on the same line, either attacking a
square beyond it or defending a piece behind an enemy piece. For example, a
rook behind an enemy rook on the same file still "sees" through it once the
enemy rook is captured.

## Desperado
Also: desperado piece; desperado
A piece that is lost anyway is used to grab as much material as possible, or
to cause damage, before it is taken. Common when both sides have pieces
hanging at once.

## Trapped piece
Also: trapped piece; trap a piece; bishop trapped; queen trapped
A piece with no safe squares can be won by simply attacking it. Classic cases:
the bishop on a7 or h7 trapped by a pawn move after grabbing a pawn, a knight
stranded on the edge, and the queen grabbing the b2 pawn ("poisoned pawn")
and getting cut off.

## Perpetual check
Also: perpetual; perpetual check; endless checks
A series of checks the defender cannot escape, so the game is drawn, in
practice by threefold repetition. A common way for a player who is behind in
material to save the game.

## Stalemate trick
Also: stalemate swindle; stalemate trap
A losing player arranges a position where they have no legal moves except
with pieces that the opponent must capture, for example a rook that keeps
checking: once it is taken, the king has no legal moves and it is stalemate,
a draw. The winning side must always check that the opponent has a move.

## Underpromotion
Also: underpromotion; promote to a knight; promote to a rook
Promoting a pawn to a rook, bishop or knight instead of a queen. Usually done
to avoid stalemate, or to promote to a knight that gives check or a fork. The
Lasker Trap in the Albin Countergambit ends with a famous knight promotion.

## Sacrifice
Also: sac; sacrifice; piece sacrifice; why sacrifice
Giving up material on purpose for something more valuable: mate, winning
back more material, a lasting attack or positional advantages. A sound
sacrifice is calculated or judged; a speculative one relies on practical
chances. Tal was the great master of sacrifices whose soundness was hard to
judge over the board.

## Greek gift sacrifice
Also: Greek gift; Bxh7+; classic bishop sacrifice
The classic bishop sacrifice on h7 (or h2 for Black): Bxh7+ Kxh7, Ng5+ and
Qh5 (or Qd3), bringing the queen and knight into a mating attack against the
castled king. It usually works when a white pawn on e5 drives the defending
knight away from f6, the knight can reach g5 safely, and the queen can join.

## Windmill
Also: windmill; see saw
A repeating sequence of discovered checks, typically a rook moving back and
forth along a rank while a bishop gives check, capturing a piece each time.
The most famous windmill is Torre against Lasker, Moscow 1925.

## Back rank mate
Also: back rank mate; back-rank checkmate
A rook or queen mates a king on its first rank when its own pawns block its
escape. One of the most common mating patterns at every level. Prevent it
with luft (moving h3 or similar) or by keeping a rook on the back rank.

## Smothered mate
Also: smothered mate; Philidor's legacy
A knight mates a king that is surrounded, smothered, by its own pieces. The
classic version is Philidor's Legacy: a queen check on g8 sacrifices the queen
(Qg8+ Rxg8), and Nf7 mates the king in the corner, with its own rook and pawns
blocking every square.

## Arabian mate
Also: Arabian mate
A rook and knight mate a king in the corner: the knight guards the rook's
square and the escape square diagonally, the rook gives mate along the edge.
One of the oldest known mating patterns.

## Anastasia's mate
Also: Anastasia's mate; Anastasia mate
A knight and rook (or queen) mate a king on the edge, with its own pawn
blocking an escape: the knight on e7 covers g8 and g6, and the rook mates on
the h-file. Named after an 1803 novel, Anastasia und das Schachspiel.

## Boden's mate
Also: Boden's mate; criss-cross mate
Two bishops on crossing diagonals mate a king, usually one castled queenside
and blocked by its own pieces. Named after Samuel Boden, who mated this way in
1853.

## Epaulette mate
Also: epaulette mate
A queen mates a king whose two side squares are blocked by its own pieces,
usually rooks, which look like the shoulder epaulettes of a uniform.

## Damiano's mate
Also: Damiano mate
A queen mates on h7 (or h2), supported by a pawn on g6, against a castled
king. Often set up by sacrificing a rook on h8 to bring the queen through.
Named after Pedro Damiano, a 16th-century author.

## Scholar's mate
Also: Scholar's mate; four move checkmate; four move mate; Qxf7 mate
1. e4 e5 2. Bc4 Nc6 3. Qh5 Nf6?? 4. Qxf7#. The queen and bishop attack f7, the
square only the king defends. Against it, Black can simply play 2...Nf6 or
3...g6 (then ...Nf6), attacking the queen and gaining time. Bringing the queen
out early this way is bad against a careful opponent.

## Fool's mate
Also: Fool's mate; fastest checkmate; two move checkmate
1. f3 e5 2. g4 Qh4#, the fastest possible checkmate. White weakens the
diagonal toward the king with two pawn moves and Black's queen mates. It only
happens if one side plays very badly.

## Légal's mate
Also: Legal's mate; Legal trap; Légal trap
A trap where a pinned knight moves anyway, sacrificing the queen, and the
minor pieces mate: 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 d6 3. Bc4 Bg4 4. Nc3 g6? 5. Nxe5! Bxd1??
6. Bxf7+ Ke7 7. Nd5#. Named after the French player Kermur de Légal, from the
1700s. Black should take the knight, losing only a pawn, instead of the
queen.

## Blackburne Shilling Gambit
Also: Blackburne shilling; Kostić gambit; shilling gambit trap
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4 Nd4?! A trap: if 4. Nxe5? Qg5! attacks the knight
and g2, and after 5. Nxf7?? Qxg2 6. Rf1 Qxe4+ 7. Be2 Nf3# is smothered mate.
Against 4. Nxd4 or calm development, Black has simply wasted time. Legend
says Joseph Blackburne won shillings from amateurs with it.

## Noah's Ark Trap
Also: Noah's Ark trap; trapped bishop Ruy Lopez
In the Ruy Lopez, after ...a6 and ...b5, Black's pawns can trap White's
bishop on b3 with ...c5 and ...c4. A typical line: 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5
a6 4. Ba4 d6 5. d4 b5 6. Bb3 Nxd4 7. Nxd4 exd4 8. Qxd4?? c5 9. Qd5 Be6 10. Qc6+
Bd7 11. Qd5 c4, and the bishop is lost. The name says the trap is very old.

## Elephant Trap
Also: Elephant trap; QGD trap; Nxd5 trap
In the Queen's Gambit Declined: 1. d4 d5 2. c4 e6 3. Nc3 Nf6 4. Bg5 Nbd7
5. cxd5 exd5 6. Nxd5?? Nxd5! 7. Bxd8 Bb4+, and after 8. Qd2 Bxd2+ 9. Kxd2
Kxd8 Black has won a piece. White's knight looks free on d5, but the knight
on d7 is not really pinned. Hundreds of years old, and still claims victims.

## Fishing Pole Trap
Also: Fishing pole; fishing rod trap
In the Ruy Lopez Berlin, 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5 Nf6 4. O-O Ng4!? 5. h3 h5!
offers the knight. If 6. hxg4? hxg4, the h-file opens and ...Qh4 threatens
mate, which often follows. White should not take the knight, or should take
it only with care. A popular online trap.

## Mating patterns
Also: checkmate patterns; mate patterns; how to checkmate; common mates
Knowing mating patterns makes mates easier to find, because the same
arrangements of pieces keep recurring: the back rank mate, the smothered mate,
Anastasia's, Arabian, Boden's, epaulette and Damiano's mates, the "kiss of
death" (a queen supported next to the king), and the two-rook ladder. When
attacking, look at which squares around the king are covered by its own
pieces.
